A.Z. Fell & Co. Est. 1800
(Antiquarian bookshop)
37 Whickber St, London


Opening Hours
I open the shop on most weekdays about 11:30 or perhaps 10 am while occasionally I open the shop as early as 8, I have been known not to open until 1, except on Tuesday, I tend to close about 5:30 pm, or earlier if something needs tending to. However, I might occasionally keep the shop open until 8 or 9 at night. You never know when you might need some light reading. On days that I am not in, the shop will remain closed. On weekends, I will open the shop during normal hours unless I am elsewhere. Bank Holidays will be treated in the usual tradition, with early closing on Wednesdays, or sometimes Fridays. (For Sundays see Tuesdays). A. Z. Fell, Bookseller.

Firstly: I knew you could back-date a review on this site, but I didn't know it went as far back as 1800. This will probably be deleted as a joke review, but I assure you, this is very real: In the Local History Museum of Bromley, we have lots of letters of one Abel Wright, and one of them, written by his friend Adam Bailey, somewhat reviews this bookshop. It reads as follows:

I have lately, on the first of this month, paid a visit to a novel bookshop in Soho, London. Its name is A.Z. Fell & Co. and, as I perceive, it has merely commenced its trade when I fortuitously happened upon it. As you are well aware, I cannot resist the fragrance of a fine, ancient tome, and I surmised that Lady Luck might have smiled upon me, from what I gleamed through the windows.
Indeed – your eyes are not deceived. Windows galore! And how numerous they were and how lofty, Abel! I should not have believed that vending books could render one such ample riches that the tax on windows causes no apprehension. However, when I conversed with the fellow who was polishing the open-sign, he but chuckled in my visage and inquired how one might derive pleasure from perusing his wares without a solid eight hours of daylight!

His collection is of a most formidable nature, I must acquaint you! Nay: I cannot, for you must witness it with thine own eyes to truly apprehend its grandeur. Yet, whilst perusing, I stumbled upon the most exquisite tome of Defoe's “The Life, Adventures and Piracies of the Famous Captain Singleton” and made to pay a half crown and a florin, when the purveyor of books, with a countenance akin to receiving East India Rupees, contorted his visage at me.
Lo, I was unaware that I had to relinquish them as remittance for their sale, he uttered unto me. Abel, I declare, I cannot convey the peculiarity with which this gentleman conducted himself. 'Twas as if he had established a bookshop – an opulent one, at that – yet lacked the art of vending books. Rather, he comported himself as if I were despoiling him!
We became embroiled in a discourse regarding the value of the tome (and I assure you, I was prepared to tender whatsoever sum he proposed, but with each demand of his that I was joyous to oblige, he ascended only to more exorbitant figures!) and I perceived his composure was on the verge of dissipating, when two handsomely attired gentlemen entered with an air of ownership.

Immediately, the proprietor relinquished his fighting spirit and assumed a pleasant countenance. Completely disregarded, I observed as the gentlemen firmly instructed him to abscond to a certain location. Their discourse was laced with cryptic language that perplexed my senses – mention of tasks and references to heaven as if it were a destination one could visit and subsequently return from. At that point, I surmised that these unfamiliar arrivals were affiliated with the secretive criminal organization from Liverpool! Consequently, I endeavoured to recede into the shadows and awaited an opportune moment to make my escape.

Now you may deem that mine experience in this establishment can not have become any stranger, but you would be mistaken. As I cast my eyes upon the exit, another very well accoutred gentleman showed up at the door, bearing what I can only presume was a posy of flowers and a rather sizeable box of chocolates. However, he did not venture inside, for he espied the two other fellows engaged in discourse with the vendor (all of whom were clad in raiment of pure white, as was he - the proprietor - whilst the latest addition to this spectacle was entirely clad in ebony and sported an uncommon pair of dusky-hued spectacles.) (At first, this did not strike me as peculiar, but for reasons I am about to disclose unto you, I opine it to be an essential addition to this tale!)

The proprietor, veritably cowed, did sally forth and spoke of hellish creatures and – as far as I could gather – claimed that they could not seize hold of his establishment for he alone prevented those demons' intrusion. At this juncture, I deemed it prudent to remain concealed in obscurity and to bide my time until this display of affairs would conclude, ere I could endeavour to depart.
But as soon as the mafia members exited, mine ears caught a voice from the unclosed casement, through which I espied a ghastly figure wrapped in a cloak and the sombre gentlemen from the entryway door earlier! And their discourse revolved around heavenly beings and infernal creatures, descending from perdition and thwarting celestial powers once the emporium of books ceased its operation.
Abel, verily I say, those initial guests at the establishment were doubtlessly the mafia, yet I surmise that there were even more ominous individuals partaking in this day's visitation - and that the merchant himself must be affiliated with a diabolical sect, for he too did converse of demons ere this second spectacle unfolded afore my discerning gaze.

I do confess, then, that I lacked the fortitude to tarry any longer, and with haste did I make my way into the thoroughfare and down Marlborough's with all possible speed! Only upon entering a carriage and regaining my breath dared I to take note that the Defoe yet remained within my grasp. I am not one to purloin, Abel, yet I deem it unwise to return there in the near future. Thus, it has found a place within my bookshelf, and whilst penning this epistle, it doth catch mine eye.

However, should you find thyself in London anon and should curiosity prevail, I can but urge you to visit that enigmatic bookseller on 37 Whickber Street.
